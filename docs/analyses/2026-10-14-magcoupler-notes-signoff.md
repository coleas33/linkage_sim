## torque_from_shear: How the coupling makes torque

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#torque_from_shear

### Before

(new in slice 2)

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Twist one ring against the other and the magnets across the gap pull each other back into line, so the rings pass torque.
2. That sideways pull, spread over the cylinder at the gap's mid radius R_g, is a shear stress σ (at its peak, here: 201400 Pa).
3. σ times the cylinder's area 2π·R_g·L, times lever arm R_g, is the endless-ring torque T_2D = σ·2π·R_g²·L (magnet ends ignored), L the overlapping magnet length.
4. The magnet count N keeps the gap radius R_g: blocks share one ring, each pole spanning τ_p = 2π·R_g/N; fewer flat blocks grow the cup.
5. σ grows with stronger magnet grades and magnetic steel behind the magnets; it falls as the gap widens or blocks fill less of each pole.

**What to change**

- Raise Axial magnet length, both rings: torque rises almost linearly; on the example design each millimetre beats a millimetre of radius. Costs: longer stack, higher shaft-joint and gearbox loads.
- Raise Ring radius: more area and lever arm; at the same magnet count the blocks spread apart, so add magnets as it grows. The cup grows too.
- Raise Magnets per ring: blocks fill more of each pole and torque usually rises, until they stop fitting; fewer usually lose torque and, with flat blocks, grow the cup.

**Watch out**

- The dashboard's pull-out torque, the most the rings are expected to pass before slipping, is T_2D corrected for the magnet ends and by a calibration factor (here: 3.159 N·m becomes 2.688 N·m).
- T_2D grows as R_g² only at fixed σ: a larger ring with the same magnet count fills each pole less, so the pull-out can even fall.

**Where this comes from**

- The torque model's equations: gap radius, pole pitch, overlapping length, area times lever arm, and the endless-ring torque
- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the planar harmonic model against exact 2D sections and real 3D rings
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: probes of 10 against 8 magnets, and of magnet length against ring radius

## harmonics: Block fill and the magnet pattern (harmonics)

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#harmonics

### Before

```text
title: Harmonic decomposition
sentence: Each ring's magnetization alternates north and south around the circle, so along the gap it is a rectangular wave, blocks separated by gaps, not a smooth sine.
sentence: Such a wave is a sum of sine waves at odd multiples of its basic frequency: harmonic n has n times as many wavelengths around the ring and an amplitude of 4/(nπ) times the remanence, scaled by sin(nπλ/2), where λ is the fraction of each pole the magnet fills (λ < 1 when there are gaps between blocks); that factor sets each harmonic's size and sign, so some amplitudes come out negative even with no gaps (sin(3π/2) = −1), and the fill decides which ones.
sentence: Harmonic n of one ring pulls only on harmonic n of the other, so the total shear stress is a sum with one term per harmonic.
sentence: Higher harmonics have shorter wavelengths and a lower amplitude ceiling, 4/(nπ) times the remanence (the fill can still make a higher one larger than a lower one), and above all their fields fade across the gap much faster, which is why the workbook keeps only 1, 3 and 5 (the calculator's default; it can sum the odd harmonics up to 11).
watch out: A fill of exactly 0.4 makes the fifth harmonic vanish, because sin(5π·0.4/2) = sin(π) = 0: that is geometry, not an error.
source: M1 audit M3, M4, M7 (the planar harmonic model against exact 2D sections)
source: Addendum A decision 29 (odd harmonics up to 11)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Around each ring, blocks alternate north and south with spaces between them, so the magnet pattern along the gap is squarish, not smooth.
2. It equals a sine wave, the fundamental (one wave per pair of poles), plus weaker odd harmonics with 3, 5, 7 times the waves.
3. The fundamental grows with sin(π·λ/2), where the fill λ is the block's width over the room each pole has on that ring.
4. Each harmonic pulls only on its match on the other ring; higher pairs usually add little, being weaker and fading faster across the gap.
5. On this design the fills are 0.8612 inner and 0.6198 outer; the fundamental alone peaks at 200000 Pa, the summed harmonics at σ = 201400 Pa.

**What to change**

- Choose Outer magnet part: a wider, equally thick outer block raises outer fill and torque if blocks fit; shaft-joint and gearbox loads rise; its grade may tolerate less heat.
- Raise Manual outer width (tangential): with custom dimensions, a wider outer block fills more of its pole and raises torque while the blocks still fit; shaft-joint and gearbox loads rise.
- Raise Magnets per ring: more blocks on the same ring fill more of each pole; they must still fit, and fewer magnets leave wider spaces between blocks.
- Choose Highest odd harmonic summed: a model setting, not a design lever: keep at least 1, 3, 5; more for narrow, widely spaced blocks, where higher harmonics can move pull-out.

**Watch out**

- At low fill, from few magnets, a large ring or narrow blocks, the third harmonic can change the pull-out by more than a tenth and move its angle.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the planar harmonic model against exact 2D sections
- The odd-harmonic set, summed up to 11
- Physics read of 2026-10-14: the third harmonic at low fill (6 magnets, a 20 mm ring radius, narrow blocks)

## back_iron_factor: Back iron: why steel behind the magnets adds torque

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#back_iron_factor

### Before

```text
title: Back iron: the sinh factor against free space
sentence: The geometry factor S_n says how much of harmonic n's field from one ring reaches through the other ring.
sentence: With steel behind both rings, the field lines cross the gap and close through the steel instead of spreading into the air behind the magnets, and the factor takes the sinh form.
sentence: The steel makes the field meet its surface at right angles, so the growing and decaying exponentials across the gap combine into sinh; in free space only the decaying one remains.
sentence: Without back iron each ring's field leaks out behind it as well as across the gap, so the free-space factor, with its decay e^(−kg), is smaller.
sentence: Both fall off with k·g, the gap measured against the harmonic's wavelength, so a small gap matters most for the high harmonics.
source: M1 audit M3 (planar factor against the cylindrical one)
source: M1 audit M5 (steel at the magnet backs)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Magnetic steel behind each ring gives the flux an easy return path, so more of it crosses the gap instead of leaking out behind.
2. The geometry factor S_n, which turns both rings' magnet strengths into pull across the gap, is therefore always larger with steel.
3. On this design S_1 is 0.2445 with magnetic steel and 0.1392 without; the gain is largest when the pole pitch is long against magnet thickness.
4. Across the gap the fundamental fades about as e^(−π·g/τ_p), g being the gap, so what matters is the gap against the pole pitch.
5. S_1 has a best pole pitch: too long for thin magnets and the field is weak; too short and it fades across the gap.

**What to change**

- Choose Back iron material (hub, cup and boss): 4140 or 1018 keep the steel circuit; 304 and aluminium switch to free space and lose much torque; 12L14, 416 and 17-4PH may saturate.
- Choose Back iron: keep the steel circuit; with no back iron the torque model uses free space, whatever material is picked.
- Lower Magnet face gap: less of the field fades across the gap, so torque rises; running clearance and room for the sleeve and liner shrink.
- Raise Manual outer thickness (radial): a thicker outer block raises S_n with shrinking gains, but it lowers the outer fill, so past a point torque falls; the cup grows.

**Watch out**

- The torque assumes the steel never saturates: where the cup wall check fails, the real coupling can fall below the torque shown.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the planar back-iron factor against the cylindrical one
- Math audit of 2026-09-29: steel at the magnet backs
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: S_1 with steel at 8, 10 and 12 magnets (its best pole pitch)

## pullout_angle: Pull-out: the most torque before the rings slip

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#pullout_angle

### Before

```text
title: Pull-out torque against rotation angle
sentence: Twist one ring against the other and the torque rises from zero, peaks and falls again: the peak is the pull-out torque, the most the coupling carries before it slips.
sentence: Harmonic n contributes A_n sin(nφ), where φ is the electrical angle, 90° at half a pole pitch.
sentence: With the fundamental alone the peak is at φ = 90°; a strong third harmonic can move it, so the calculator takes the angle where the summed curve is highest (correction E7).
source: M1 audit E7 (T3-RC2)
source: Addendum A decision 29 (one peak search for any harmonic set)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Pull-out torque is the peak of torque against twist: the most the coupling is expected to pass before the rings slip, a nominal estimate.
2. It is computed at the magnet temperature while running (here: 50.00 °C) as T_pull = T_2D·f_end·f_cal: the endless-ring torque times end and calibration factors.
3. Angles in this model are electrical: φ = N/2 times the mechanical twist, so 90° is half a pole pitch, a twist of 180°/N.
4. The fundamental alone peaks at 90°; at low fill a same-sign third harmonic above about a ninth of it shifts the peak below 90°.
5. Loaded past it, the rings slip: torque then swings between plus and minus pull-out once per pole pair passed, averaging only a small drag.

**What to change**

- Raise Axial magnet length, both rings: pull-out grows almost linearly with the overlap; the stack lengthens, and shaft-joint and gearbox loads rise.
- Choose Inner magnet part: a same-size, stronger-grade block raises pull-out proportionally to its built-in flux density Br unless calibration changes; shaft-joint and gearbox loads rise; heat tolerance often drops.
- Lower Operating magnet temperature: the pull-out is reported at this temperature; lower it only if the magnets really run cooler, or the estimate is optimistic.

**Watch out**

- A slipping coupling may keep slipping after the load drops below pull-out: it locks again only once the slip speed falls, and meanwhile it heats.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the pull-out is the highest point of the summed curve, not always half a pole pitch
- One peak search for any harmonic set
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: the same-sign third harmonic above a ninth; electrical against mechanical angle

## end_effect: Magnet ends: the length lost to fringing

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#end_effect

### Before

```text
title: End effect
sentence: The 2D model treats the rings as infinitely long, but real magnets end, and near each end the field fringes outward and carries less torque.
sentence: The factor f_end = 1 − c_end·τ_p/L takes off a total of about c_end pole pitches, half at each end, from the active length L.
sentence: It is empirical: at the default design it is within about 1 % of 3D for magnets longer than about 3 mm (about a third of a pole pitch), it has no gap term (one M1 skeptic found it 11 % high at a 4.4 mm gap), and it turns negative for very short magnets, which the end-effect check flags.
watch out: At or below L = c_end·τ_p (1.3 mm at the default pole pitch) the factor is zero or negative and every torque computed from it is meaningless; between that and about 3 mm the check still reads OK, yet the factor is far off (21 % below 3D at 2 mm, M1 audit M9).
source: M1 audit M8 and M9 (T3-RC1, T3-RC3)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Near each magnet end some of the field fringes outward instead of crossing the gap, so the ends carry less torque than the middle.
2. The model counts this as missing length: f_end = 1 − c_end·τ_p/L, with c_end an empirical coefficient, τ_p the pole pitch, L the magnet overlap.
3. It takes c_end·τ_p, half at each end, off L; here f_end is 0.8960, τ_p 8.809 mm and L 12.70 mm.
4. So torque grows in step with L − c_end·τ_p: every extra millimetre of overlap adds the same torque, and the share lost shrinks.
5. Steel-free 3D puts the example design's f_end within 3 % from 3.2 mm; with magnetic steel behind the magnets, the default, f_end likely reads high.

**What to change**

- Raise Axial magnet length, both rings: longer magnets lose a smaller share at the ends and add torque; the stack grows, and shaft-joint and gearbox loads rise with cold-high.
- Raise End-effect coefficient: only with 3D or bench evidence; every torque falls, cold-high and the shaft-joint and gearbox loads included. The bench prototype keeps its own coefficient.

**Watch out**

- Its end loss barely grows with the gap: in a 3D check at a 4.4 mm gap it read 11 % high, so treat wide-gap torque as optimistic.
- At short overlap f_end is least reliable and can read too high or too low; at or below c_end·τ_p there is no prediction and the end-effect check flags it.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the end factor against free-space 3D rings, at wide gaps and for short magnets
- Physics review of 2026-10-15: the end factor against free-space 3D by magnet length (within 3 % from 3.2 mm), by magnets per ring and at a 20 mm ring radius

## calibration: Calibration: what scales the torque estimate

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#calibration

### Before

```text
title: The one-point calibration
sentence: The 2D harmonic model reads a few per cent low against real flat blocks (about 4 % without back iron, M1 audit M4; less with steel, M5), so the workbook scales it by a calibration factor: an assumed f_cal,0 (0.95, which adds conservatism rather than correcting that bias) or one measured on the bench.
sentence: When a design has the measured prototype's circuit (no back iron), pole count and magnet part (B842SH on both rings), it uses the bench result: f_cal,1 = f_cal,0 · T_meas / T_model, which puts the model of the prototype on the 1.8 N·m measurement.
sentence: Because T_model already contains f_cal,0, the assumed factor cancels: the bench correction is T_meas divided by the uncalibrated model, whatever f_cal,0 was.
sentence: Every other design keeps the assumed 0.95, since one measurement cannot say how the model's error changes with the design; the rule does not compare gaps or radii, so a resized design with the prototype's circuit, pole count and magnet part still gets the bench factor.
watch out: The bench value has two significant figures and an assumed 20 °C test temperature; it sits about 1 % above the 3D pull-out at the corrected remanence of 1.30 T (correction E3), and 2.5 % above it at the audit's 1.29 T (M1 audit P2).
source: M1 audit M4, M5 and M6 (the model's bias without and with back iron, and the calibration that absorbs it)
source: M1 audit P2 (the bench value against physics)
source: M1 audit E3 (the N42SH remanence corrected from 1.29 T to 1.30 T, Calibration!C21 included)
source: Plan A-3 traceability: the f_cal,0 cancellation (tests/explain.rs CANCELLATIONS)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. The calculator multiplies its torque model by a calibration factor; for most designs that factor is an assumed allowance, not a measurement.
2. The only bench test it holds is of a prototype without steel, so for a design backed by magnetic steel the factor is a judgement.
3. A design gets the measured factor only with no magnetic steel behind the magnets, the prototype's magnets per ring and B842SH parts on both rings.
4. The measured factor makes the prototype's modelled torque match its bench measurement; the rule ignores ring radius, gap and magnet length.
5. Entering or leaving that match steps every torque between the two factors, by rule, not physics; the factor in use here is 0.9500.

**What to change**

- Lower Original calibration coefficient: every torque outside a prototype match falls in proportion, cold-high included; a match ignores it, and no steel bench test backs any value.
- Enter Measured pull-out torque: the measured bench pull-out, never a value for your design; the measured factor rises with it, and only a prototype match uses it.
- Enter Assumed test magnet temperature: the recorded bench magnet temperature; a warmer test weakens the modelled prototype, so the measured factor rises.

**Watch out**

- The prototype inputs describe that one bench test: enter a real test's values, never edit them to match a new design, which gives a meaningless factor.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the model's bias without and with back iron, and the calibration that absorbs it
- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the bench value against physics
- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the N42SH remanence corrected from 1.29 T to 1.30 T, the prototype's included
- Traceability check: the assumed factor cancels out of the bench correction
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: the step on leaving a match; the rule compares no size

## br_temperature: Heat weakens magnets: torque against temperature

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#br_temperature

### Before

```text
title: Remanence against temperature, and torque ∝ Br²
sentence: A magnet's remanence Br, the flux density it keeps with no applied field, falls reversibly as it warms: Br(ϑ) = Br(20 °C) · (1 + α (ϑ − 20 °C)), with α about −0.12 %/°C for sintered NdFeB.
sentence: Each ring's field is proportional to its own Br, and the shear stress is a product of the two rings' fields, so the torque scales as Br,i · Br,o: the square of Br when both rings are one grade.
sentence: So a 30 °C rise costs about 7 % of the torque: (1 − 0.0012 · 30)² ≈ 0.93.
sentence: The loss is reversible: cool the magnet and the torque comes back, unless on the way it passed a demagnetization onset, the temperature where its reverse field reaches the knee, which is a separate, permanent loss; the calculator's demagnetization limit sits 10 °C below the lowest onset, the one while the coupling skips.
watch out: Each ring keeps its own coefficient: a ferrite ring (α about −0.2 %/°C) loses torque faster with heat than an NdFeB one.
source: M1 audit, confirmed: the Br(T) and torque-temperature rows (Calculator C69, C70, C94; Metal design C8)
source: Addendum A decision A2-7 (each ring with its own coefficient)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Magnets lose strength reversibly as they warm, so the pull-out torque depends on the magnet temperature.
2. Remanence Br, a magnet's built-in flux density, falls by a fixed share of its 20 °C value per °C: the coefficient α.
3. Torque is proportional to inner Br times outer Br, so each °C of warming changes it by a share of about α_i + α_o.
4. Here the pull-out is 2.688 N·m at 50.00 °C and 2.893 N·m at 20 °C: the dashboard's two pull-out rows.
5. Each torque has its own temperature: hot-low the operating one, cold-high the minimum, the hot-day torque ambient plus the driving rise.

**What to change**

- Raise Operating magnet temperature: set it to the magnet temperature while running; hotter lowers the pull-out and hot-low, not the 20 °C row.
- Raise Reversible Br temperature coefficient: a value nearer zero changes torque less with temperature, hot and cold. Take it from the datasheet; rings with a picked grade ignore it.
- Choose Inner magnet grade (manual dimensions): a grade whose coefficient is nearer zero loses a smaller share when hot; SmCo, the nearest, starts weaker than sintered NdFeB. Custom dimensions only.
- Raise Hot-day ambient temperature: a hotter day lowers the hot-day torque and the temperature margins; the hot-day start is this ambient plus the driving rise.

**Watch out**

- Library magnets share one coefficient input; only a ring with custom dimensions and a picked grade uses its grade's own. In use here: inner -0.001200 1/°C, outer -0.001200 1/°C.
- The loss is reversible only inside the magnet's limits: too hot for NdFeB, or too cold for ferrite, and it loses strength for good.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29, confirmed: the remanence and torque-against-temperature rows
- Design ruling: a ring with custom dimensions and a picked grade uses its grade's coefficient; library parts share one input
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: which temperature each torque uses; the two pull-out rows

## hot_low_cold_high: Hot-low and cold-high: the torque band

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#hot_low_cold_high

### Before

(new in slice 2)

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Hot-low is the weakest coupling expected at the operating temperature, and cold-high the strongest at the minimum temperature, allowing for production scatter.
2. Hot-low, here 2.285 N·m, is the pull-out at the operating temperature less the variation allowance; it must reach the requirement.
3. Cold-high, here 3.823 N·m, is the pull-out at the minimum temperature plus that allowance: the most torque expected before it slips.
4. Whatever holds the hub on the shaft must carry cold-high with margin, and the gearbox output carries it too, so added torque loads both.

- Lower Minimum magnet temperature: a colder minimum raises cold-high and with it the shaft-joint and gearbox loads; enter the coldest the magnets really get.

**Watch out**

- Hot-low uses the operating temperature you enter, not the hot-day start (65.00 °C here); the hot-day torque is reported apart, without the allowance.
- Nothing checks cold-high against the gearbox: at its input it is 0.8049 N·m here; compare that with the gearbox's input rating yourself.

## demagnetization: Demagnetization: permanent loss from heat and slipping

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#demagnetization

### Before

```text
title: Demagnetization: knee, permeance and the onset temperatures
sentence: Inside a magnet the demagnetizing field H points against its magnetization (B still points along it); the stronger this reverse field, the closer the magnet is to losing magnetization for good.
sentence: The loss starts at the knee of the intrinsic curve, taken as H_k = 0.9 · Hcj, and Hcj falls as NdFeB heats (β about −0.5 %/°C), so each reverse field has an onset temperature where the falling knee meets it; the reverse field itself shrinks with Br(T), which is why the onset needs both α and β.
sentence: The reverse field depends on the magnet's surroundings, its permeance: a reference magnet on the load line B = −μ0 H (permeance coefficient 1) sees Br/(2 μ0), and like poles of the other ring facing it while the coupling skips push it highest, 863 kA/m here, so the skipping onset sets the limit.
sentence: The onsets are shifted so the reference magnet reaches its knee exactly at its rated temperature, and the design limit keeps a 10 °C margin below the skipping onset.
sentence: With correction E20 each ring is checked with its own grade, and the ring with the lower limit governs.
watch out: The default N42SH's temperature coefficients hold over 20–150 °C only: onsets above 150 °C are extrapolations, and so is the reference magnet's model knee near 160 °C that sets the shift, so a ±20 % change of the Hcj slope above 150 °C moves even the default limit by up to about 3 °C (M1 audit M13).
source: M1 audit M13 (knee temperature outside the coefficient range)
source: M1 audit rulings: the onset calibration (it uses 9.83 °C of the 10 °C margin)
source: Addendum A decision 19 (E20: each ring's own grade)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Too much heat combined with a strong opposing field can weaken a magnet for good, unlike the loss with temperature that cooling undoes.
2. A field pushing against the magnet's direction, a reverse field, does no lasting harm until it passes the knee, where the magnet stops springing back.
3. Heat lowers an NdFeB magnet's knee, so each reverse field has an onset temperature; above it, part of the magnetization is lost for good.
4. Slipping passes like poles face to face, the worst reverse field; its onset, here 103.1 °C, sets an NdFeB ring's limit.
5. Onsets are shifted to match the magnet's rated maximum temperature; each NdFeB ring's limit is its slipping onset less a margin; the lower limit governs.

**What to change**

- Choose Inner magnet part: custom dimensions and an NdFeB grade rated hotter usually raise this ring's limit; those above N42SH are weaker, cutting torque, and the adhesive may govern.
- Choose Outer magnet part: the same for the outer ring: the lower of the two limits governs, so a hotter grade on one ring alone may not help.
- Enter 3D worst reverse field, like poles facing: enter it from a 3D field calculation of your design, which needs an outside solver; the stored value belongs to the example design.

**Watch out**

- The reverse fields are stored from the example design's 3D model: they ignore magnet count, ring radius, gap, block size and grade, reading low for stronger grades.
- At their grade's own rating, onsets for N52, N50, N50M, N38UH, N35EH and 2:17 SmCo are shifted upward: limits may read optimistic; onsets past a grade's tested range are extrapolated.

**Where this comes from**

- Math audit of 2026-09-29: the knee temperature outside the coefficient range
- Math audit of 2026-09-29: other calibration forms move the skipping onset by up to 9.83 °C
- Design ruling: each ring checked with its own grade
- Guidance audit of 2026-10-13: the stored reverse fields; the rating shift raises the onsets of grades rated above their model knee temperature

## ferrite_cold_demag: Ferrite magnets: demagnetization risk at the cold end

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#ferrite_cold_demag

### Before

```text
title: Ferrite: the demagnetization risk is at cold
sentence: In hard ferrite the coercivity rises as it warms (β about +0.35 %/°C), the opposite of NdFeB, so its knee falls as the magnet cools.
sentence: Its remanence still rises as it cools, so the reverse field grows while the knee shrinks: below some temperature the reverse field passes the knee and the magnet loses magnetization.
sentence: The calculator finds that cold onset, ϑ = 20 + (H_k − H)/(H α − H_k β), and checks the minimum magnet temperature against the skipping cold onset plus the 10 °C margin; heating moves a ferrite magnet's knee away from the reverse field, so its rating is the hot limit.
watch out: A ferrite ring can pass every hot check and still fail at −40 °C: read the cold check too, and enter the ferrite design's own reverse fields, since the defaults (354 to 863 kA/m) come from the NdFeB default design and put a Y30 ring's cold limit at 336 °C.
source: Spec A6 (ferrite's opposite-sign beta gets a teaching note)
source: Addendum A decisions 3 (Y30 beta +0.35 %/°C) and 19 (E20's positive-beta branch)
source: A-1 plan decision A13 (both rings; the cold side from the ring with the higher cold limit)
```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Ferrite magnets risk permanent loss when cold, not hot: cooling lowers their knee while their own field, and so the reverse field, grows.
2. Below a cold onset the reverse field passes the knee, and the ring loses part of its magnetization for good; slipping sets that onset.
3. The cold limit is that onset plus a margin; the coldest the magnets get must stay at or above it, on both rings.
4. Heat moves a ferrite knee away from the reverse field, so a ferrite ring's hot limit is simply its rated maximum temperature.

- Choose Outer magnet part: the same for the outer ring; the cold check passes only if both rings pass.

**Watch out**

- The default reverse field for slipping, from the NdFeB example design, exceeds a Y30 knee at every temperature Y30 is rated for, so the check always fails with it.

## when_slipping: When the coupling slips

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#when_slipping

### Before

(new in slice 2)

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Past pull-out the coupling slips: one ring turns against the other, and the torque between them, carried by the gearbox, swings back and forth.
2. The swing runs from plus to minus the pull-out once per pole pair passed, so the load gets almost no torque on average.
3. What remains is a drag from eddy currents, which the moving field drives in the metal parts, heating them: here an estimated 0.01314 N·m.
4. The rings re-lock only once the slip speed falls, so slow the drive; a load dropping just below pull-out is not enough.
5. Each swing puts like poles face to face, the magnets' hardest case, and sends torque ripple to the gearbox: at its input, here 0.5660 N·m.

**What to change**

- Lower Slip fault trip time (unbroken slip): a shorter unbroken slip heats the magnets less; detect slip and slow the drive within it, without tripping on brief slips.
- Lower Relative slip speed: slower slipping makes less heat and slower swings, here 166.7 Hz; the drive's behaviour after slip sets it, not the coupling.

**Watch out**

- Nothing checks the gearbox against these swings: when cold they can reach the cold-high torque, on this design 0.8049 N·m at its input; check its rating.
- The drag, the heating and the magnets' limits use fields stored from the example design's 3D model; those fields do not follow your geometry or magnet grade.

**Where this comes from**

- The torque-angle model: pull-out as the peak of the summed harmonics, one cycle per pole pair
- The slip-loss and thermal model: the drag, the heating and the slip fault trip time
- The demagnetization model: the like-poles-facing reverse field while slipping sets the magnet limit
- The gearbox rows: the ripple at the gearbox input is the pull-out through the gear ratio and efficiency

## slip_heating: Slip heating: where the heat comes from

Review: docs/analyses/2026-10-14-magcoupler-notes-review.md#slip_heating

### Before

```text
title: Eddy-current slip loss and skin depth
sentence: When the coupling slips, each ring's alternating field sweeps past the other ring's parts at the field frequency f_e = p · n / 60, with p the pole pairs and n the slip speed in rev/min, and by Faraday's law it drives eddy currents in every conductor it crosses: the steel cup and hub, the sleeve, the liner, the cap and the magnets themselves.
```

## thermal_time_constant: Slip temperature: how hot, and how fast

### Before

```text
title: The thermal time constant
sentence: The calculator treats the rotating coupling as one lump with heat capacity C = Σ m c, losing heat to its surroundings through one conductance G.
sentence: Heated with power P, its temperature rises toward ϑ_start + P/G along ϑ(t) = ϑ_start + (P/G)(1 − e^(−t/τ)), with time constant τ = C/G: 63 % of the rise after τ and 95 % after 3τ.

```

### Now (default design)

**What it is**

1. Unbroken slip heats the coupling toward a steady rise set by loss and heat path; mass changes only how fast, which the time constant measures.
2. One lump stands for the rotating parts: heat capacity C, conductance G to its surroundings, starting at the hot-day start, hot ambient plus driving rise.
